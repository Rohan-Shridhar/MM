Analyzing Buyer Behaviour II

From consumer perception and attitudes to organizational buying and the strategic realities of B2B marketing.

On this page
IntroductionSummaryCore ConceptsKey TakeawaysQuiz

What You Will Learn

This week links the way consumers perceive goods and services and form attitudes with the distinct processes organizations use to purchase for business. You will examine service expectations, derived demand, buying-center roles, buying situations and life cycle cost (LCC).

These ideas matter because individual choices depend on perception and context, while B2B purchases often involve multiple stakeholders, formal evaluation, and costs over the product’s entire life. Together, they help marketers tailor value and evidence to the buyer and purchase situation.

Summary — Consolidated Lecture Notes

Goods–services continuum

Most market offerings combine tangible goods and intangible services. The continuum helps marketers understand what customers are actually buying and which parts of the offer need emphasis.

Offering typeTypical characteristicsExample
Pure tangible goodPrimarily physical, with little accompanying service.A basic packaged commodity.
Tangible good with servicesA physical product supported by delivery, installation, warranty, or repairs.A laptop with setup and technical support.
Hybrid offeringGoods and services are both central to the experience.A restaurant meal.
Service with accompanying goodsThe service is central, supported by physical items.An airline flight with a meal and amenities.
Pure servicePrimarily intangible performance or expertise.Consulting or tutoring.

Search, experience, and credence qualities

  • Search qualities: Can be assessed before purchase, such as size, colour, or listed specifications.
  • Experience qualities: Are easier to assess during or after use, such as comfort or the taste of a meal.
  • Credence qualities: May remain difficult to judge even after consumption, such as whether a complex repair was truly necessary.
Remember: Search = before use; experience = during/after use; credence = difficult to verify even afterward.

Consumer perception and attitudes

The tripartite attitude model

The model divides attitude into three connected components:

Cognitive — Think

Beliefs and knowledge about an offering. Example: “This laptop has a long battery life.”

Affective — Feel

Emotional reactions or evaluations. Example: “I like this laptop’s design.”

Conative — Act / intend

Behavioural intention or tendency to act. Example: “I intend to buy this laptop.”

Marketing use

Marketers can address beliefs with evidence, feelings with brand experience, and intentions with convenient calls to action.

Attitude formation and influence

  • Direct experience can strengthen or change beliefs and feelings.
  • Family, peers, culture, and reference groups can shape attitudes.
  • Advertising and reviews provide information that consumers interpret through existing beliefs.
  • Attitudes influence behaviour, but they do not guarantee it: price, availability, time, and situational constraints can intervene.

Service expectations and the zone of tolerance

Customers often have a desired service level—the service they hope to receive—and an adequate service level—the minimum they consider acceptable. The range between them is the zone of tolerance.

Below adequate

Service falls short of the minimum acceptable level, creating dissatisfaction.

Within the zone

Service is acceptable, although it may not be exceptional.

At or above desired

Service meets or exceeds expectations and can create strong satisfaction or delight.

Why it matters

Expectations vary by customer, context, urgency, and past experience; service delivery should account for these differences.

Business-to-Business (B2B) marketing

B2B marketing involves organizations purchasing goods and services to use in production, operations, resale, or service delivery. Industrial buying often differs from consumer buying in scale, formal procedures, technical requirements, and the number of people involved.

Key characteristics

  • Derived demand: Demand for business inputs depends on demand for the final goods and services they support.
  • Fewer, larger buyers: Business markets may have fewer customers, with each account representing substantial value.
  • Professional purchasing: Buyers may use formal specifications, tenders, budgets, and supplier assessments.
  • Multiple decision-makers: Technical, financial, operational, and management stakeholders may all influence the choice.
  • Longer relationships: Reliability, support, service levels, and trust can be as important as price.
  • Demand fluctuations: Changes in consumer demand can affect suppliers throughout the business supply chain.

Example of derived demand

If demand for electric vehicles rises, manufacturers may need more batteries, charging components, and production equipment. Demand for these industrial inputs is derived from demand for the final vehicles.

B2B marketing focus: Demonstrate measurable business value, technical suitability, reliability, service support, and total cost—not merely a low purchase price.

The organizational buying center

A buying center is not necessarily a formal department. It includes the people who participate in or influence a particular organizational purchase. One person may perform more than one role.

RoleResponsibilityExample
InitiatorFirst identifies the need or purchase idea.An engineer requests new testing equipment.
UserUses the product or service.Technicians who operate the equipment.
InfluencerProvides technical or other evaluation criteria.An IT specialist assesses compatibility.
DeciderChooses or approves the supplier or product.A department head approves the selection.
BuyerHandles supplier selection, negotiation, and purchasing procedures.A procurement officer issues the purchase order.
GatekeeperControls access to information or contact with decision-makers.An assistant schedules vendor meetings.

How to market to a buying center

  • Identify all stakeholders and their priorities.
  • Provide technical evidence to influencers and users.
  • Demonstrate financial value and risk reduction to decision-makers.
  • Make purchasing procedures, contracts, delivery, and support straightforward for buyers.
  • Provide clear, relevant information through gatekeepers and approved channels.

Organizational buying situations

New task

The organization buys a product or service for the first time. Uncertainty and perceived risk are high, so information search and evaluation are extensive. Most complex.

Modified rebuy

The organization has bought before but wants to change specifications, price, supplier, delivery, or other conditions. Moderate evaluation.

Straight rebuy

The organization routinely reorders a familiar product from an established supplier with little change. Most routine.

Marketing implication

New-task buyers need education and risk reduction; modified-rebuy buyers need a clear reason to switch; straight-rebuy buyers value consistency and dependable service.

Life cycle cost (LCC)

Life cycle cost is the total cost associated with a product throughout its useful life, not just the amount paid at purchase.

Purchase+Installation+Operation+Maintenance+Disposal

Illustrative example: Machine A costs ₹5 lakh initially but requires expensive energy and frequent repairs. Machine B costs ₹5.8 lakh but uses less energy and needs fewer repairs. Comparing only purchase prices may favour A; comparing total life cycle costs may show B is the better-value choice.

Exam point: Industrial buyers evaluate the total economic impact over time. The cheapest initial option is not necessarily the lowest-cost option overall.

Key Takeaways

  • The goods–services continuum ranges from pure goods to pure services.
  • Search qualities can be checked before buying; experience qualities are assessed through use; credence qualities may remain difficult to verify.
  • The tripartite attitude model is cognitive (think), affective (feel), and conative (act/intend).
  • The zone of tolerance lies between desired and adequate service levels.
  • B2B demand is often derived from demand for final consumer products.
  • The buying center includes initiators, users, influencers, deciders, buyers, and gatekeepers.
  • New task = first-time purchase; modified rebuy = purchase with changes; straight rebuy = routine reorder.
  • Life cycle cost includes purchase, installation, operation, maintenance, and disposal.
Study strategy: For scenario questions, identify whether the focus is a consumer attitude, service expectation, organizational stakeholder, buying situation, or total cost over time.

Quiz / Practice Questions

These 10 practice questions were supplied separately from the original Week 6 lecture notes; their wording, options, answer keys, and explanations are retained.

1. A customer reads online reviews and compares several cab-booking apps before selecting one. Which stage is represented by this comparison?

Ans: a — The pre-purchase stage occurs before buying a service and includes searching for information and comparing alternatives.

2. A consumer considers Coca-Cola, Sprite, and a new lemonade brand before choosing a drink. What is the set of these considered brands called?

Ans: d — The evoked set consists of the brands a consumer actively considers when making a purchase decision.

3. A customer checks a restaurant's menu, location, and prices before visiting. After eating, the customer judges the taste and service but cannot verify whether the cooking oil was healthy. Which classification is correct?

Ans: b — Search attributes can be evaluated before purchase, experience attributes after consumption, and credence attributes remain difficult to verify even afterward.

4. An attractive advertisement for a new energy drink creates a favorable opinion of the brand before the customer has used it. Which attitude model is illustrated?

Ans: c — The attitude-toward-the-ad model explains how consumers' reactions to an advertisement influence their attitudes toward the advertised brand.

5. A customer regularly buys a toothpaste brand because it cleans effectively and protects against cavities. Which attitude function is represented?

Ans: b — The utilitarian function concerns practical benefits and usefulness, such as effective cleaning and cavity protection.

6. A household buys a refrigerator for personal use rather than resale or commercial production. What type of market transaction is this?

Ans: d — Consumer markets involve products purchased for personal or household consumption.

7. An aircraft-engine manufacturer sells customized engines to one national airline. Several departments participate in the lengthy buying process, and demand depends on passenger travel demand. Which combination of B2B characteristics is illustrated?

Ans: a — The scenario involves few customers, customized products, multiple decision-makers, and derived demand based on demand for airline travel.

8. A company sells customized industrial water-filtration systems to a few chemical plants. It uses its own technical sales team and maintains long-term service relationships. Which combination of marketing program characteristics is shown?

Ans: d — B2B marketing commonly involves technical products, direct sales teams, a narrow customer base, and long-term customer relationships.

9. An employee suggests new project-management software, IT evaluates alternatives, top management approves the purchase, and procurement places the order. Who is the decider?

Ans: c — The decider has the authority to make or approve the final purchase decision.

10. A large company purchases office equipment through a formal process involving finance, operations, and procurement before final approval. Which aspect of industrial buyer behavior is described?

Ans: b — Purchasing organization refers to the formal structure and involvement of departments in the buying process.
