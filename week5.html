Analyzing Buyer Behaviour

Understand how consumers recognize needs, search for information, evaluate alternatives, make purchases, and respond after purchase.

On this page
IntroductionSummaryCore ConceptsKey TakeawaysQuiz

What You Will Learn

This week follows the consumer journey from need recognition through post-purchase evaluation, then explains how involvement and information sources affect the process. You will connect socio-cultural and psychological influences to alternative evaluation rules, purchase risk and cognitive dissonance.

Understanding the whole process helps marketers design relevant products, messages and experiences while recognizing that customers, consumers, social context and purchase situations can differ.

Summary — Consolidated Lecture Notes

Buyer behaviour and market context

Consumer behaviour studies the acts and decision processes involved in obtaining, using, and disposing of products and services. Understanding how consumers make these decisions helps marketers design products, messages, and experiences that fit customers’ needs and build lasting relationships. Buyer behaviour is shaped by the market context, the roles of customers and consumers, the stages of buying, and socio-cultural, psychological, and situational influences.

Core idea: Effective marketing requires understanding the whole consumer journey—from the first recognition of a need to the feelings and evaluations that follow the purchase.

Customer versus consumer

Customer

The person or organization that purchases the product or service. Example: A parent buying a school bag.

Consumer

The person who ultimately uses or consumes the product or service. Example: The child who uses the school bag.

The customer and consumer can be the same person, but they do not have to be. Consumer satisfaction remains important to long-term success.

Common market contexts

  • Business-to-Consumer (B2C): Products are bought for personal or household use.
  • Industrial / business markets: Products and services are bought to support business operations.

The five-stage buyer decision-making model

The basic buying model describes a typical journey. Consumers may move quickly through some stages or revisit stages depending on involvement, experience, and the purchase situation.

1. Need recognition→2. Information search→3. Evaluation→4. Purchase→5. Post-purchase
StageWhat happens?Example
1. Need RecognitionThe consumer recognizes a gap between the current and desired state.A damaged backpack is no longer convenient for college.
2. Information SearchThe consumer looks for ways to satisfy the need.Searching online and asking friends about backpack brands.
3. Evaluation of AlternativesThe consumer compares available options using relevant attributes.Comparing price, durability, and customer ratings.
4. Purchase DecisionThe consumer selects and buys an option.Choosing a backpack and deciding where and when to buy it.
5. Post-Purchase BehaviourThe consumer evaluates the experience against expectations.Feeling satisfied or disappointed after using the backpack.
Exam clue: Comparing different brands is evaluation of alternatives; looking for reviews and information is information search.

Consumer involvement and information search

Consumer involvement refers to the perceived importance or risk of a purchase. It influences how much effort consumers invest in gathering and processing information.

High involvement — Know → Think → Act

  • Extensive information search.
  • Common in unfamiliar or important purchases, such as a first car.
  • Often described as new-task buying or extended problem-solving.
  • Marketing: educational advertising and strong personal selling.

Low involvement — Know → Act → Think

  • Limited or little information search.
  • Common in routine purchases, such as milk.
  • Often associated with straight re-buy or habitual buying.
  • Marketing: wide distribution and reminder advertising.

Modified re-buy

A moderate-involvement situation in which the buyer has previous experience but wants to change some aspect of the purchase. This usually involves limited problem-solving.

Information sources

SourceExamples
PersonalFriends and family.
CommercialAdvertisements and salespeople.
PublicConsumer organizations and public information.
ExperientialTrying or directly using the product.
Infomediary: An intermediary that collects, organizes, or provides consumer information. Comparison websites can help consumers compare products and may provide aggregated insights to businesses.

Socio-cultural influences on buyer behaviour

Culture and subculture

Culture is learned and dynamic. It influences what people want, how they behave, and which products they accept. For example, food customs or childcare preferences may differ across countries, so a product or message that works in one market may need adaptation.

Subcultures may be based on nationality, religion, ethnicity, or region. Marketers may need to tailor products and communication to relevant differences.

Social factors

  • Socio-economic classification: Factors such as occupation, education, and assets can relate to purchasing power and consumption patterns.
  • Reference groups: Formal or informal groups that influence beliefs, lifestyles, attitudes, and purchases. These can include aspirational groups people want to join and dissociative groups they wish to avoid identifying with.
  • Family of orientation: The family a person grows up in, such as parents and siblings, which shapes core beliefs.
  • Family of procreation: The family formed as an adult, such as a spouse and children, which often influences everyday consumption.
  • Role and status: Social roles and perceived status can influence product and brand choices.
Marketing implication: Social and cultural context affects not only what people buy, but also how they interpret a product’s meaning and suitability.

Psychological influences

Motivation

Motivation is the force that initiates and directs action toward a goal or need.

  • Freudian theory: Suggests that subconscious motives can influence choices beyond the reasons consumers state openly.
  • Maslow’s hierarchy of needs: Organizes needs from physiological needs through safety, social belonging, esteem, and self-actualization.
  • Herzberg’s two-factor theory: Distinguishes hygiene factors, whose absence can cause dissatisfaction, from motivators that can create satisfaction.

Perception

Perception is how consumers select, organize, and interpret information. Three selective processes are especially important:

  • Selective attention: Noticing only some stimuli, often those relevant to current interests or needs.
  • Selective distortion: Interpreting information in ways that fit existing beliefs.
  • Selective retention: Remembering information that supports existing preferences or beliefs.
  • Subliminal perception: Processing stimuli below the level of conscious awareness; its influence should not be overstated.

Learning

  • Classical conditioning: Learning through association, such as repeatedly pairing a brand with a positive stimulus.
  • Instrumental conditioning: Learning shaped by outcomes, rewards, or consequences, such as repeat buying after a good experience.
  • Cognitive learning: Active, knowledge-based learning and problem-solving, common in high-involvement purchases.

Memory

Memory stores information through associations. Brand recall and recognition can influence which options enter a consumer’s consideration set.

Evaluation of alternatives

Attitude and bounded rationality

An attitude is a learned predisposition to respond favourably or unfavourably. Consumer attitudes are influenced by beliefs about attributes, intentions, and behaviour.

Bounded rationality means consumers make decisions within limits such as available information, time, and cognitive capacity. Metagoals are higher-level goals that guide the choice process, such as maximizing accuracy or minimizing effort.

Compensatory versus non-compensatory models

Model / ruleHow it worksExample
Compensatory modelStrong performance on one attribute can offset weak performance on another.A phone with a higher price may still win because its camera and battery are excellent.
Fishbein multiattribute modelCombines attribute importance with the consumer’s rating of each brand to form an overall evaluation.Assign weights to camera, battery, storage, and price, then calculate an overall score.
Non-compensatory modelA product that fails a critical minimum standard may be rejected regardless of other strengths.A buyer rejects a phone if storage is below a required minimum.

Four non-compensatory decision rules

  1. Conjunctive rule: Reject any brand that falls below the minimum cut-off on any important attribute.
  2. Disjunctive rule: Accept a brand if it meets a cut-off on at least one critical attribute.
  3. Elimination by aspects: Rank attributes by importance and eliminate options that fail the cut-off for each attribute in sequence.
  4. Lexicographic rule: Choose the brand that performs best on the most important attribute; use the next most important attribute to break a tie.
Memory aid: Conjunctive = pass all minimums; Disjunctive = pass at least one key minimum; Elimination by aspects = filter step by step; Lexicographic = best on the top-ranked attribute.

Purchase and post-purchase evaluation

Purchase decision

The purchase decision includes more than selecting a brand. Consumers may also choose the store, quantity, purchase time, and payment method. Situational factors and perceived risk can change or delay a planned purchase.

Perceived risk

  • Functional risk: The product may not perform reliably.
  • Financial risk: The purchase or future upkeep may cost more than expected.
  • Social risk: Others may judge the choice negatively.
  • Psychological risk: The choice may conflict with the consumer’s self-image or create discomfort.
  • Time-loss risk: The product may waste time or require inconvenient effort.

Post-purchase evaluation

  • Satisfaction: Performance meets expectations.
  • Dissatisfaction: Performance falls short of expectations.
  • Delight: Performance exceeds expectations.

Cognitive dissonance

Cognitive dissonance is mental discomfort from a conflict between beliefs, attitudes, or behaviour after a purchase. Consumers may reduce it by finding new supportive information, changing their beliefs, or reducing the importance of the conflicting issue.

Marketing implication: Clear product information, realistic promises, helpful onboarding, and responsive after-sales support can reassure customers and reduce post-purchase doubt.

Key Takeaways

  • Consumer behaviour includes obtaining, using, and disposing of goods and services.
  • The five stages are need recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, purchase decision, and post-purchase behaviour.
  • High involvement usually means deeper search; low involvement often means habitual buying.
  • Infomediaries organize consumer information and comparisons.
  • Culture, subculture, reference groups, family, and social status influence buying behaviour.
  • Motivation, perception, learning, and memory are important psychological influences.
  • Compensatory models allow trade-offs; non-compensatory rules apply cut-offs.
  • The lexicographic rule prioritizes the most important attribute.
  • Perceived risk can be functional, financial, social, psychological, or time-related.
  • Cognitive dissonance is post-purchase discomfort caused by conflicting beliefs or behaviour.
Study tip: In scenario-based questions, identify the behaviour described first—searching, comparing, choosing, or justifying after purchase—then match it to the stage or concept.

Quiz / Practice Questions

Questions and options are transcribed from the source Assignment 5 material. Correct answers and explanations are retained.

Q1. A student notices that the current backpack has become damaged and inconvenient for regular college use. The student then explores various online shopping platforms, checks multiple brands by comparing features such as price, durability and customer ratings and finally purchases a suitable backpack. After using it for a few days, the student feels happy with the choice made. Which stage of the buyer decision-making process is represented when the student compares different backpack brands before making the purchase?

Correct answer: c) Evaluation of Alternatives. The student compares brands on price, durability, and ratings before choosing one.

Q2. A retail store purchases the same brand of cooking oil from a familiar supplier every month. Since the quality has remained reliable over the years, the store owner places the order routinely without comparing other brands or suppliers. Which type of buying situation is being described?

Correct answer: b) Straight Re-buy / Habitual Buying. The store repeatedly buys the same product from the same supplier without comparing alternatives.

Q3. A customer visits a website to compare prices, features and user reviews for several digital cameras before making a purchase. The website collects the customer's search behavior and preferences, then sells this anonymous data to camera manufacturers who want to understand what features customers value most. The website itself does not sell cameras directly. What role is the website playing?

Correct answer: c) Infomediary. An infomediary collects, organizes, or provides consumer information to businesses.

Q4. A college student recently started spending time with a group of fitness enthusiasts who regularly share workout videos, healthy eating habits, and active lifestyle updates on social media. Over time the student also began exercising regularly, changed eating habits and started purchasing sportswear brands preferred by the group in order to fit in. Which concept best explains the influence of the group on the student's behaviour?

Correct answer: a) Reference Group Influence. Reference groups influence attitudes, lifestyle, and buying behaviour.

Q5. An employee is unhappy at work due to poor salary structure, uncomfortable working conditions and unclear company policies. Even though the work itself is fine, these issues are causing dissatisfaction. Which type of factors are mainly responsible for this dissatisfaction?

Correct answer: d) Hygiene Factors. Salary, working conditions, company policies, and job security are hygiene factors; poor hygiene factors can cause dissatisfaction.

Q6. A consumer watches multiple advertisements while scrolling on a mobile app. The consumer only notices the ads for products currently of interest and ignores the rest completely, even though all ads are clearly visible. Which concept best explains this behavior?

Correct answer: b) Selective Attention. Consumers notice information relevant to their current interests while ignoring other visible stimuli.

Q7. A consumer is comparing three smartphone brands before making a purchase decision. The consumer evaluates each brand based on important attributes such as battery life, camera quality, storage capacity and price. After assigning importance to each attribute and rating the brands accordingly, the consumer selects the brand with the highest overall score. Which concept best explains this consumer's attitude formation process?

Correct answer: d) Fishbein Multiattribute Model of Attitude. The model combines attribute importance with brand ratings to form an overall evaluation.

Q8. A customer is comparing different smartphone brands before making a purchase decision. The customer considers camera quality as the most important feature and immediately chooses the phone with the best camera performance, even though some competing phones may offer better battery backup or storage capacity. Which decision-making rule is the customer using?

Correct answer: d) Lexicographic Rule. The consumer chooses the brand that performs best on the most important attribute.

Q9. A working professional considers buying an electric scooter for everyday office travel. Although the person initially intends to purchase it, the decision is postponed at the final moment due to concerns about vehicle reliability during long rides, worries about social image among peers and uncertainty regarding future maintenance expenses. Which set of perceived risks most appropriately explains the consumer's hesitation?

Correct answer: a) Functional risk + Social risk + Financial risk. Reliability concerns are functional risk, peer image concerns are social risk, and maintenance costs are financial risk.

Q10. A consumer strongly believes that homemade food is healthier than restaurant food. However, due to lack of time, frequent ordering from outside continues. Over time, the consumer starts justifying this behavior by convincing themselves that certain restaurants also serve healthy and hygienic meals, which reduces the discomfort. Which stage of Cognitive Dissonance (Linear Model) is being described when the consumer starts convincing themselves that restaurant food can also be healthy?

Correct answer: c) Dissonance Reduced. The consumer justifies the behaviour by finding reasons to believe restaurant food can also be healthy, reducing psychological discomfort.
