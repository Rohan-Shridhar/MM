1. Introduction: Capturing Market Insights

In a hyper-competitive business environment, gathering, processing, and acting on relevant market insights is essential for organizational survival and growth.

Imagine a company launching a product without understanding its target audience, competitors, or broader market trends. This would be like sailing without a compass. Businesses need systematic ways to turn raw data into useful knowledge so they can make better decisions.

This week introduces the evolution from traditional Marketing Information Systems (MIS) to the more comprehensive Marketing Information and Knowledge Systems (MIKS). The emphasis is not merely on collecting information, but on interpreting it to understand customers, competitors, and internal capabilities.

Core idea: Successful marketing moves beyond collecting data. It turns data into interpreted knowledge that supports decisions, anticipates market changes, and helps build customer advocacy.

2. From data to knowledge

Data is the starting point, not the final answer. It must be placed in context and interpreted before it can guide action.

Data→Information→Knowledge→Action / Decision

Data

Raw facts, figures, or statistics. On their own, they may not explain what is happening.

Information

Data organized or placed in context so that patterns and meaning become clearer.

Knowledge

Information interpreted for action, producing a plan or decision framework.

Action

Using knowledge to make a marketing decision, respond to changes, or identify an opportunity.

Remember: More data does not automatically mean better decisions. Context, interpretation, and human judgment are needed to create useful knowledge.

3. Key terminology

TermDefinitionSignificance
Marketing Information and Knowledge System (MIKS)An evolved concept of MIS that explicitly includes generating and applying knowledge, recognizing that raw data and information must be interpreted for strategic action.Highlights the role of human intuition and interpretation in turning information into actionable knowledge for modern marketing.
DataRaw, unorganized facts, figures, or statistics that may not convey significant meaning in isolation.The building blocks of marketing insights; data needs context to become useful.
KnowledgeMeaningful information interpreted for action, leading to a clear plan or decision framework.The goal of MIKS, enabling effective and strategic action in the marketplace.
Management Decision Problem (MDP)A broad, action-oriented question faced by a manager, often related to symptoms such as declining sales or market share.The starting point for research because it defines the manager’s need for action.
Marketing Research Problem (MRP)A specific, information-oriented question defining what information is needed to address the management decision problem.Turns a business challenge into a researchable question and guides information gathering.
Exploratory ResearchA research design focused on exploring and understanding a problem, often used when the problem is not clearly defined.Helps sharpen the problem definition, identify alternatives, and gain initial insights using qualitative methods.
EthnographyAn exploratory research technique that observes customers in their natural environments, drawing from anthropology.Uncovers latent needs and cultural influences by studying how products and services fit into everyday life.
Latent NeedsUnstated or subconscious needs that consumers may not be aware of or able to articulate directly.Discovering these needs can inspire breakthrough product innovations and effective marketing strategies.

4. Management Decision Problem (MDP) vs Marketing Research Problem (MRP)

AspectMDPMRP
Main focusWhat should the manager do?What information is needed, and how can it be obtained?
OrientationAction-orientedInformation-oriented
ScopeUsually broad and based on a business symptom.Specific and researchable.
Example“How can we reverse declining sales?”“Which factors are causing the target segment’s purchase frequency to fall?”
Easy way to remember: MDP = the decision the manager must make. MRP = the information researchers need to provide to support that decision.

5. Exploratory research

Exploratory research is useful when a business problem is unclear or not yet precisely defined. Its purpose is to build an initial understanding rather than immediately test a fully formed hypothesis.

What it helps with

  • Clarifying and refining the research problem.
  • Discovering possible explanations for a business issue.
  • Identifying alternatives or opportunities that need further investigation.
  • Generating initial insights, often through qualitative methods.
Example: If sales are falling but the reason is unknown, exploratory research might begin with open-ended customer interviews or observation to discover possible causes before designing a larger study.

6. Ethnography and latent needs

Ethnography

Ethnography involves observing customers in their natural surroundings to understand their routines, behaviours, and cultural context. It can reveal differences between what people say they do and what they actually do.

Latent needs

Latent needs are not always directly expressed. Customers may not recognize a problem clearly or may struggle to describe the solution they want. Observing everyday behaviour can help uncover these hidden needs.

Connection: Ethnography is one way to discover latent needs because it examines how people actually live, work, and use products or services in context.

7. Quick revision

  • MIS → MIKS: Moves beyond information systems by emphasizing the creation and application of knowledge.
  • Data: Raw facts; context is needed for meaning.
  • Knowledge: Interpreted information that guides action.
  • MDP: A manager’s action-oriented decision problem.
  • MRP: The specific information problem that research must answer.
  • Exploratory research: Helps clarify an unclear problem and discover initial insights.
  • Ethnography: Observes people in their natural environment.
  • Latent needs: Unspoken needs that may lead to valuable innovation.
