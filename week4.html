What You Will Learn

Building on exploratory research, this week moves toward conclusive research: establishing relationships, quantifying phenomena, testing hypotheses, and making predictions. You will connect research design and causality with measurement scales, sampling, hypothesis testing and appropriate analysis methods.

These methods make marketing decisions more systematic and evidence-based: they help isolate effects, collect consistent information, choose participants, and interpret results.

Summary — Consolidated Lecture Notes

Conclusive research and causality

Understanding consumer behaviour and market dynamics is a strategic necessity. After exploratory research, conclusive research establishes relationships, quantifies phenomena, tests hypotheses, and supports predictions.

Marketing decisions become more reliable when supported by a systematic research process rather than intuition alone. This requires identifying cause-and-effect relationships, designing measurement instruments, selecting samples, and applying appropriate analytical techniques.

Core idea: Move from initial discovery to rigorous measurement and evidence-based conclusions that help marketers test strategies, optimize programs, and predict outcomes.

Research design

Choose an appropriate approach to answer the research question.

Causality

Data analysis

Use statistical techniques to turn observations into strategic evidence.

Causality and experimental validity

Correlation is not causation. Two variables may change together because of a third factor, without either variable directly causing the other.

Example: Ice cream and soft-drink sales may both rise during summer. The seasonal weather can influence both, so their correlation alone does not prove that one causes the other.

Internal validity

Internal validity concerns whether the observed effect can confidently be attributed to the variable being tested rather than other factors. In an advertising experiment, controlling price, store location, and other promotions helps isolate the effect of the advertisement.

Factorial design

A factorial design studies two or more factors at the same time and examines both their individual effects and their combined effects. For example, a company may study price and advertising together to assess their separate and joint effects on sales.

Measurement and scales

Measurement assigns numbers or symbols to characteristics; a scale determines what those values represent and what comparisons are meaningful.

ScaleWhat it representsExample
NominalCategories with no order or ranking.Choosing a favourite brand from a list.
OrdinalCategories with a meaningful order, but gaps between ranks are not necessarily equal.Ranking brands from most to least preferred.
IntervalOrdered values with equal intervals, but no meaningful absolute zero.Temperature measured in Celsius.
RatioEqual intervals and a meaningful zero, so ratios are meaningful.Sales revenue, time, or number of purchases.

Questionnaires

A questionnaire is a formal set of questions designed around research objectives. Questions should be relevant, clear, and consistently presented so responses can be interpreted reliably.

Quick memory aid: Nominal = name/category; Ordinal = order; Interval = equal intervals; Ratio = real zero.

Sampling

Studying every member of a population may be impractical. Sampling selects a smaller group so researchers can draw conclusions about the larger population, provided the sample and method are appropriate.

Probability sampling

  • Simple random sampling: Each population member has a known chance of selection.
  • Stratified sampling: Divide the population into groups (strata) and randomly select participants from each group.
  • Cluster sampling: Divide the population into clusters and select clusters for study according to the sampling plan.

Non-probability sampling

  • Convenience sampling: Select participants who are easiest to reach.
  • Judgmental sampling: The researcher selects participants based on informed judgment.
Assignment example: Randomly selecting customers from each defined group is stratified sampling, a probability method.

Hypothesis testing

A hypothesis is a statement that can be assessed using data.

Null hypothesis (H₀)

States that there is no effect or difference; it is the default assumption.

Alternative hypothesis (H₁ / Hₐ)

States that an effect or difference exists.

Example: A company expects a new pricing strategy to increase sales compared with the current strategy. This expected increase is the alternative hypothesis.

Type I error

A Type I error occurs when a true null hypothesis is rejected. It is a false positive: the researcher concludes that an effect exists when it does not.

Remember: The researcher's expectation that a new strategy will improve sales is not the null hypothesis; it is the alternative hypothesis.

Data analysis techniques

TechniquePrimary purposeExample / clue
ANOVACompare means across two or more groups.Check whether average sales differ across several groups.
MANOVA / MANCOVAExamine effects of independent variables on two or more dependent variables simultaneously; MANCOVA also accounts for covariates.Advertising spend, price, and distribution intensity related to both brand attitude and purchase intention.
Factor analysisReduce many correlated variables to a smaller set of underlying factors.Find dimensions that explain responses to many brand-perception questions.
Cluster analysisGroup cases based on similarity.Segment customers into groups with similar characteristics.
Conjoint analysisEstimate how people value combinations of product attributes.Identify preferred combinations of battery life, camera quality, storage, and price.

RegressionModel relationships to predict or explain an outcome.Assess how one or more predictors relate to a dependent measure.

Key Takeaways

  • Conclusive research moves from discovery toward testing, quantification, and prediction.
  • Correlation alone does not establish causality.
  • Internal validity improves when alternative explanations are controlled.
  • Factorial designs examine multiple factors and their combined effects.
  • Nominal data are categories without ranking; ordinal data have order.
  • Sampling studies a subset rather than every member of a population.
  • H₀ states no effect/difference; H₁ states an effect/difference.
  • Type I error = rejecting a true null hypothesis.
  • ANOVA compares means; MANOVA/MANCOVA handles multiple dependent variables.
  • Factor analysis reduces variables; cluster analysis groups cases; conjoint analysis studies attribute preferences; MDS maps perceived similarities.
Study tip: For application questions, identify the key clue in the scenario first—cause/effect, categories, group selection, multiple outcomes, feature trade-offs, or a perceptual map—then match it to the correct concept.

Quiz / Practice Questions

Questions and options follow the source Assignment 4 material. The stray citation marker in the source introduction has been omitted as non-academic markup.

Q1. A company observes that as ice cream sales increase, soft drink sales also increase during summer. However, one does not directly cause the other. What does this relationship represent?

Answer: d) Correlation. Correlation means two variables change together, but it does not necessarily mean one causes the other. Summer conditions can affect both sales.

Q2. A researcher conducts an experiment to study the effect of a new advertisement on product sales. The researcher carefully controls other factors such as price, store location and promotions to make sure that only the advertisement influences the results. Which aspect of experimental validity is the researcher primarily trying to achieve?

Answer: b) Internal Validity. Controlling other factors helps isolate whether the advertisement caused the observed effect.

Q3. A company studies the effect of two factors (price and advertising) at the same time and analyzes both individual and combined effects on sales. Which statistical design is used here?

Answer: a) Factorial Design. It studies two or more factors simultaneously, including their individual and combined effects.

Q4. A survey asks customers to choose their favorite brand from a list of options without any ranking. Which scale of measurement is used here?

Answer: d) Nominal Scale. The response identifies a category without assigning any order or rank.

Q5. A company selects only 200 customers out of 10,000 to study their buying behavior. Which method is used here?

Answer: d) Sample. The 200 customers are a smaller subset selected from the population of 10,000 customers.

Q6. A researcher divides customers into groups and randomly selects from each group. This is an example of:

Answer: b) Stratified Sampling (Probability). The population is divided into strata and participants are randomly selected from each group.

Q7. A company believes that a new pricing strategy will increase sales compared to the current strategy. What is this belief called?

Answer: c) Alternate Hypothesis. The alternative hypothesis states that an effect or difference exists; here, the expected effect is increased sales.

Q8. A marketing researcher wants to analyze the impact of advertising spend, price and distribution intensity (independent variables) on both brand attitude and purchase intention (dependent variables) simultaneously. Which multivariate dependence technique is most appropriate?

Answer: b) MANOVA/MANCOVA. This approach is suited to studying multiple dependent variables simultaneously; MANCOVA additionally accounts for covariates.

Q9. A smartphone company studies how consumers value different product attributes such as battery life, camera quality, storage capacity, and price in order to identify which combination of features customers prefer most. Which marketing research technique is being used in this situation?

Answer: d) Conjoint analysis. It helps estimate how customers value individual attributes and combinations of product features.

Q10. A company creates a visual map showing how customers perceive different smartphone brands based on features such as price, quality and innovation. Brands that are perceived as similar appear closer together on the map. Which marketing research technique is being used in this situation?

Answer: c) Multidimensional Scaling (MDS). MDS creates a perceptual map where brands perceived as similar appear closer together.
